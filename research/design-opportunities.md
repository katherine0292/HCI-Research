# Designing for task resumption

The field study points to a design objective for mobile work. When someone returns from an interruption, help them identify the item or action that still needs attention. The concepts below are hypotheses from the analysis.

## Concepts and evidence

| Concept | Evidence from the study | Interaction hypothesis | What to evaluate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Resume marker** | The participant reopened a folder and repeated the address and document-type check after losing her place. See P-31–P-34. | Preserve the last confirmed step and show the next unresolved one when the worker returns. Leave ambiguous progress open for confirmation. | Correct next action, unnecessary rechecks and time to resume after a call. |
| **Temporary placement cue** | Keys beside the cup holder and a folder by the printer escaped the usual bag check. See P-05–P-14. | Give the worker an easy way to mark that an essential item is temporarily outside its usual container. Surface that exception during departure. | Recovery before departure, time to record the placement and prompts caused by intentional exceptions. |
| **Final-action check** | A prepared bill felt complete until a tenant's message prompted an email check. See P-46–P-49. | Show completion after the final send or submission is confirmed. Surface unresolved drafts at a relevant return or departure moment. | Unfinished tasks caught before handoff, duplicate actions and the effort required to dismiss irrelevant prompts. |
| **Pause at a natural checkpoint** | The planned door check caught a wrong key set. The participant reported that the eraser prompted a pause in a different incident. See P-16–P-19 and P-52. | Attach a light cue to a movement or location that the person already visits. Use it as a moment to check the next action. | Whether the prompt fits the routine, is acted on while rushed and continues to be useful over time. |

## Proposed evaluation

I would start with the resume marker and final-action check because the folder and bill incidents expose an information gap that an interface can make visible. A paper or clickable prototype could model a work visit with keys selected, paperwork partly checked and a message prepared for sending.

In moderated sessions with people who do mobile administrative work, I would ask each participant to complete comparable scenarios with and without the cue. I would vary when the interruption arrives and rotate condition order to reduce learning effects. Tasks would include both a real unfinished step and a completed task to see whether the prototype creates unnecessary rechecking. Observation would focus on the first action after the interruption, the first cue the participant notices and whether they trust the progress state.

The primary measures would be correct resumption, missed final actions and time from return to a correct action. I would also record unwanted prompts, duplicate actions and the reported cost of maintaining the cue. If the cue helps in controlled scenarios, a short field pilot could examine whether it remains useful amid real interruptions, whether workers override it and which placements or task states are too costly to track.
