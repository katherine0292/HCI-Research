# When the Body Remembers

**Team interview study · 15 participants · five independent coders · 356 code entries**

## Research question

How do people remember belongings when moving between everyday places? We examined how a person decides what to take, checks readiness to leave and notices a missing item. Prospective memory means remembering to carry out an intended action later. In this study, those intentions included taking an item when leaving or retrieving it after using it somewhere away from home.

## My contribution

My documented work includes two interview records and their coding sheets. The first case concerned a mobile worker who left a charger at a shared workspace after a conversation broke the packing sequence. The second concerned a worker who relied on fixed pocket positions and a tactile check for work keys. Together, the two coding sheets contain 54 entries, 32 in the first and 22 in the second.

I used descriptive coding to capture the action or condition in each extract and analytic coding to explain its role in remembering or recovery. For example, in the charger case I distinguished the physical experience of reaching into an empty sleeve from the interpretation that a routine reach can become a detection cue. The other coders' interview work, the combined register, the clustering and the final synthesis are credited to the team.

## Method

The semi-structured guide covered everyday belongings, departure sequences, bodily checks, environmental cues and experiences of forgetting. Asking for a specific incident made it possible to reconstruct what happened before departure and what made the problem apparent. Follow-up questions addressed where the item was, what the participant's hands were doing and how the episode differed from their usual routine. See the [team interview guide](../methods/research-instruments.md#team-interview-guide).

The team consolidated 356 code entries into 26 clusters, then reviewed them as six themes. The register preserved provisional and final assignments. During theme review, 17 entries changed theme when their clusters were kept together. The team used contributions from at least three coders as a criterion for carrying a cluster forward on its own. Two narrower clusters, learning through costly loss and relying on familiar people, were retained as supporting evidence within the consequences theme. Coder coverage records whose work contributed to a theme. Each participant count below is the number of distinct participants with at least one entry in that theme in the final register.

## Findings

| Theme | Code entries | Participants represented | What the evidence showed |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| **A container or fixed location reduces what people have to remember** | 65 | 15/15 | A bag, assigned pocket or designated spot can stand for several things at once. Some participants pared down what they carried to simplify the check. |
| **Items outside the routine are easier to overlook** | 70 | 14/15 | An item charging at a socket, sitting in yesterday's bag or needed only for one outing can fall outside the usual check. Familiar places could carry risk when people unpacked their normal set. |
| **People check through movement and sensation** | 61 | 14/15 | Pocket pats, weight, shape, sound and a last look around a seat helped people verify that they were ready to leave. These cues often verified the container or a familiar position. |
| **Omissions often surface at a later checkpoint** | 45 | 15/15 | An empty pocket, an unexpectedly light bag or the first moment of need exposed what was missing. Some people built checks into their route, such as at a doorway or turnstile. |
| **Competing demands can displace the check** | 72 | 15/15 | Hurry, conversations and caring responsibilities could take up the time or movement normally used to check. Two useful details were whether a hand was occupied and whether the departure sequence had changed. |
| **Consequences shape which items people protect** | 43 | 14/15 | The anticipated cost of losing an item shaped whether people labelled it, assigned it a fixed place or checked it repeatedly. Misplacing a less consequential item could be tolerable. |

The underlying cluster totals and coverage are available in the [interview cluster summary](../evidence/interview-clusters.csv).

## What the exceptions clarified

One interviewee described packing correctly while absorbed in conversation. Another reported that conversation had never caused a problem for them. These examples qualified the interruption theme. I took forward the more specific question of what happens when an interruption occupies the hand needed for the check or displaces the next movement. Other accounts showed that a check tied to standing up or pushing in a chair could survive hurry. At a familiar venue, confidence that staff would recognise a forgotten item could also change how closely someone checked.

## Why I ran a follow-up

An interview can reconstruct a remembered incident. It is harder to separate the moment the routine changed, the moment the person noticed and the moment they reported it. I designed a field probe to capture short reports close to the event when possible, then used a debrief to fill in the sequence. I chose a participant with frequent transitions and visible consequences for checking errors so I could examine practical recovery cues such as a glance, a missed expected touch or a planned check before opening a door. See the [field probe study](field-probe-study.md).
